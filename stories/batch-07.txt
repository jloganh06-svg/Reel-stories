=== 32 book-club ===
My wife made me quit my band to stay home weekends. Her Thursday book club has no books. Am I wrong?

I'm 34. My wife Celeste is 33. We've been married four years, together six. She's a dental office manager, she's organized, and she's the most persuasive person I have ever met.

For nine years I played drums in a bar band called the Low Tides. Nothing fancy. Covers, a few originals, Friday nights at a place by the river. I made maybe $80 a night, and I loved it more than anything I've ever done.

Celeste used to come to every show. She'd stand right by the stage and sing along to all of it.

Then last spring, she sat me down at the kitchen table with a notepad. She had actual bullet points.

She said, "Grady, married men don't go out every Friday night. It looks bad. People talk. My mom has already asked me if we're okay."

I said, "It's a gig. You used to come."

She said, "I came because I was your girlfriend. I'm your wife now. I need you home. If you can't see that, I don't know what we're doing."

Then she cried. Not loud. Just one tear she let sit there while she looked at me.

So I quit. I told the guys at practice. Sully, our bass player, didn't talk to me for a month.

A few weeks later, my drum kit was gone from the garage. Celeste said she sold it to a coworker's nephew for $600, because it was "just collecting dust and making me sad."

Around that same time, Celeste joined a book club. Thursday nights. She'd leave at six and get home after midnight.

I didn't think about it much. Until I noticed the book on her nightstand. Same book, same bookmark, page forty one, for five straight months.

Then one Thursday she came home with her voice almost gone, glitter on her neck, and her hair stiff with hairspray.

And her phone lit up on the counter. A text from someone saved as Dex.

"You killed it tonight. Same time next week. Don't tell him."

Would you have asked her about Dex that night, or followed her next Thursday? Follow for part 2.
--- part ---
Part 2 of my wife making me quit my band while her Thursday book club kept her out past midnight.

I didn't say anything that night. Celeste could turn any question into proof that I was jealous or controlling, and I didn't want to hear another speech about trust.

So I waited a week.

Thursday at six, she kissed me on the cheek and grabbed a tote bag. The paperback was sitting on top, like a prop.

She said, "Don't wait up. We're doing the last three chapters."

I said, "Of the book you've been on page forty one of since May?"

She laughed and said, "You're cute," and left.

I gave her ten minutes and drove after her. I expected a house, or an apartment, or a bar. Some guy named Dex standing in a doorway.

She drove forty minutes to a banquet hall out by the highway. The sign out front said congratulations Marla and Ben.

It was a wedding.

I parked in the back of the lot and sat there feeling like an idiot. Then I heard it through the walls. A band. A good one. Bass so loud the windows buzzed.

And the singer was my wife.

I knew it before I even got to the door. I've heard her sing in the car a thousand times. I walked up to the glass and looked in.

Celeste was on a little stage in a sequined black dress, belting out a song I used to play every Friday. Behind her was a keyboard player with a shaved head and a vest. The drummer was a kid who looked about twenty.

The banner behind them said Velvet Hour. Weddings, Corporate, Private Events.

I stood there through two whole songs. People were dancing. She was amazing. I'm not even going to pretend she wasn't.

Then the drummer hit a crash cymbal, and I saw the bass drum head.

It had my initials on it. The ones I painted myself in silver when I was twenty five.

She hadn't sold my kit to a coworker's nephew. Her band was playing it.

Would you have walked in right then, or waited for her to come home? Follow for part 3.
--- part ---
Part 3. My wife made me quit my band, and she's secretly singing in a wedding band on my drums.

I didn't walk in. I went home and sat at the kitchen table, the same one where she'd made her bullet points, and waited.

She got home at 12:40. Hoarse. Glitter on her collarbone.

I said, "How was book club?"

She said, "Good. Long. We argued about the ending."

I said, "Marla and Ben seemed happy."

She stopped with one shoe off.

Then she did the thing she always does. She didn't deny it. She reframed it.

She said, "Okay, yes, I sing with a band. But it's completely different. It's a job, Grady. I get paid $350 a night. You were out partying with your buddies for eighty bucks."

I said, "You told me married people don't go out on weekends."

She said, "Thursdays aren't weekends."

I said, "That banner said weddings. Weddings are on Saturdays."

She didn't answer that.

I asked who Dex was. She said he's the keyboard player and the band leader, and he's sixty one and has grandkids, and I was being ridiculous. Honestly, I believed her on that part.

Then I asked about my drums.

She said, "They needed a kit, and you weren't using it."

I said, "Because you made me quit."

She said, "You chose to quit. I just asked."

The next morning her mother called me. Not Celeste. Her mother. She said Celeste had been crying all night, that I'd followed her like a stalker, and that a husband should be proud his wife has a talent.

I said, "I am proud. I'd just like the same deal."

She hung up on me.

That afternoon I opened our joint account to see where $350 a night had been going. Fourteen months of gigs. Not one deposit.

Then I found a second bank login saved on our laptop. An account I'd never heard of, with a balance of $11,200. The account nickname was "my exit fund."

Would you have confronted her about the account, or quietly called a lawyer first? Follow for the final part.
--- part ---
Final part of my wife making me quit my band while she sang on my drums. I found her exit fund.

I didn't confront her about the account. Not yet. I took screenshots, I emailed them to myself, and I called Sully.

I said, "I'm sorry I disappeared. I need to ask you a huge favor."

He said, "You want back in."

I said, "I want back in."

He said, "Friday. Bring sticks. We'll figure out the rest."

Then I looked up Velvet Hour's website and found Dex's number. I called him and told him who I was. I was ready for him to hang up.

Instead he was quiet for a second, then he said, "Son, she told us you gave her that kit. She said you quit drumming because you lost interest."

I told him everything. The bullet points. The tear. The $600 coworker's nephew who didn't exist.

He said, "Come get your drums Saturday. I'll find the kid another kit. And I'm sorry."

Saturday morning I loaded my kit into my truck in Dex's driveway. Celeste found out an hour later when the drummer texted her asking what was going on.

She came home furious. She said I'd humiliated her in front of her band.

So I put the laptop on the table and opened the account called my exit fund.

I said, "You made a rule that married people stay home. You broke it every week. You sold my drums to yourself. And you've been saving to leave me with money from the job you told me was book club."

She said, "That account is for emergencies."

I said, "It's named my exit fund, Celeste."

She didn't have anything for that one.

We're separated now. My lawyer says the exit fund counts as marital money, so half of it is coming back around. Celeste still sings with Velvet Hour, and honestly, I hope she's great.

And Friday nights, I'm back by the river with the Low Tides. The bass drum still has my initials on it. Sully added a sticker underneath that says Fridays are mine.

So, was I wrong to take the drums back the way I did?
=== 33 the-webinar ===
My coworker took credit for my project. Then I found it in a competitor's webinar, with her face on it.

I'm 29, and I work in sales operations at a mid size software company. I build the dashboards and forecasts that tell our sales team where the money is coming from.

Last year, I spent four months building a forecasting model. It predicted which customers were about to cancel, with about 80 percent accuracy. My manager Alan called it the best thing our team had made in years.

Then he paired me with Gwen.

Gwen joined about eight months earlier as a senior analyst. Very polished. Camera always off on calls, which she blamed on bad internet. She'd answer messages two or three hours late and say, "Sorry, I was heads down."

Alan asked her to help me present the model at the quarterly all hands. Two hundred people on the call.

The night before, Gwen asked me to send her the final deck so she could "practice her transitions." I sent it.

The next morning, she started the presentation before I even unmuted. She went through all twenty two slides by herself, in a calm, confident voice, saying "I built" and "my model" the entire time.

At the end, the CEO said, "Gwen, this is outstanding work."

I said, "Just to add, I built the model, and Gwen..."

And she talked right over me. "Tessa helped a ton with the data cleanup. Couldn't have done it without her."

Data cleanup.

Afterward, I messaged her. She said, "We're a team. It doesn't matter whose name is on it. Don't be territorial, it's not a good look."

When I told Alan, he said, "I know it was mostly you. But fighting about it in public makes you both look bad. Let it go."

So I let it go.

Then three weeks later, my friend at another company sent me a link to a sales webinar from one of our biggest competitors. She said, "Isn't this your thing?"

I clicked it. It was my model. My slides. My colors.

And the presenter, with her camera very much on, was Gwen.

Would you have gone straight to your manager, or watched the whole webinar first? Follow for part 2.
--- part ---
Part 2 of my coworker stealing my project, then presenting it in a competitor's webinar.

I watched the whole webinar. All forty seven minutes. I took notes like it was a final exam.

On the screen, her name tag said Gwen, Director of Revenue Strategy. Director. At our company she was a senior analyst.

She was presenting my model as something her team had built over the past year. She used my exact slide order. She used my chart of customer cancellations by month, with the same blue and orange I picked because they're my college colors.

Then she got to slide fourteen.

When I build sample data for demos, I never use real customer names. I make up fake companies. Usually I name them after my dog. His name is Pickles.

On slide fourteen of the competitor's webinar, the example customer was Pickles Logistics Incorporated.

I actually laughed out loud. Alone, at my desk, at nine at night.

Then I checked the date of the webinar. It was recorded on a Tuesday afternoon. I pulled up my calendar. That same Tuesday afternoon, Gwen and I had been in a two hour planning meeting together. She'd had her camera off the entire time. When Alan asked her a question halfway through, she didn't answer for almost a minute, then said her internet was cutting out.

She wasn't having internet problems. She was in a different meeting. For a different company.

I looked her up online. Her public profile listed only our company. But the competitor's website had a team page, and there she was. Same headshot. Different last name. A hyphenated version.

I sat there doing the math. Camera always off. Messages answered hours late. Heads down.

Gwen didn't just steal my project. She had two full time jobs, at two competing companies, at the same time. And she was taking my work from one and selling it to the other.

I knew if I went to Alan, he'd tell me to let it go again.

So I didn't go to Alan.

Would you have gone over your manager's head, or confronted Gwen first? Follow for part 3.
--- part ---
Part 3. My coworker secretly works full time for our biggest competitor, and she's presenting my project there.

I went to our legal team. Not HR. Legal.

I wrote it all up in one email. The webinar link with time stamps. Screenshots of my original files, with creation dates from months before Gwen ever touched them. Our shared calendar from that Tuesday. And slide fourteen, Pickles Logistics, side by side with my demo file where I'd made up the same fake company.

A lawyer named Corinne called me forty minutes later. She said, "Don't talk to Gwen. Don't talk to Alan. Don't delete anything."

So for two weeks, I acted normal. That was the hardest part.

Gwen kept being Gwen. She asked me to send her my new quarterly forecast because she wanted to "get ahead on it." I asked Corinne what to do. She said, "Send it. We've added something."

I didn't know what that meant until later.

Then Alan pulled me aside. He'd heard something was going on with legal. He wasn't happy.

He said, "Tessa, did you go around me?"

I said, "I came to you first. You told me to let it go."

He said, "About a credit dispute. Not this."

I said, "It was always this. Nobody looked."

He went quiet. Then he said, "For what it's worth, she told me you were difficult to work with. She said you got possessive about your work. I believed her."

That one stung, honestly.

The next week, I got a calendar invite. A meeting with Gwen, Alan, Corinne, and our head of security. Gwen thought it was a project check in. The invite said Forecast Review.

When I joined, Gwen's camera was off, like always.

Corinne said, "Gwen, could you turn your camera on for this one?"

There was a long pause. The little circle with her initials just sat there.

Then Corinne said, "Before you do, I should mention we've already spoken with your other employer."

Would you have shown up to that meeting, or let legal handle it alone? Follow for the final part.
--- part ---
Final part of catching my coworker working a secret second job at our competitor and presenting my project there.

Gwen turned her camera on. For the first time in eight months, I saw her face on one of our calls.

She was in a home office with two laptops open side by side. She closed one of them very slowly.

Corinne didn't raise her voice once. She just laid it out. The webinar. The calendar. Slide fourteen. Then she explained what she'd meant about adding something.

The forecast I'd sent Gwen two weeks earlier had a fake customer buried in it. Not Pickles this time. A made up company that existed nowhere else in the world. Our competitor had emailed that exact company name to their sales team three days later. Their own legal team found it and called ours.

Gwen said, "This is a misunderstanding. A lot of people do two jobs now."

Corinne said, "Plenty of people do. Not for direct competitors, while handing one company's files to the other."

Gwen said, "Tessa and I collaborated on that model."

And I finally said it. Out loud, on camera, in front of the people who'd told me to let it go.

"You told me it doesn't matter whose name is on it. So why did you change your last name for the other job?"

She didn't answer. She left the meeting about a minute later.

She was let go from both companies by the end of the week. I don't know what happens with the legal side, and honestly, I don't want to.

Alan apologized to me in person. A real apology. He also put my name on the model in our internal wiki in big letters, and at the next all hands, the CEO gave me the credit Gwen had taken. Two hundred people. My name this time.

The model got rolled out to the whole company in the spring. I got promoted to senior analyst. Gwen's old job.

And every demo I build now still has one fake customer in it. Pickles Logistics Incorporated. My dog has never been more employed.

So, was I wrong to go to legal instead of giving her one more chance?
